## ABSTRACT 

We propose a Reinforcement Learning (RL) method to directly optimize the faith-fulness of self-explanations - the extent to which a model’s generated reasoning accurately reflects its internal decision-making process. While existing work fo-cuses on evaluating faithfulness or using inference-time prompting frameworks to improve an LLM’s self-explanation’s tractability, these approaches do not provide a mechanism to directly optimize a model’s parameters to generate faithful self-explanations. We bridge this gap by modifying existing faithfulness metrics into an RL training objective. We investigate (1) if models can be trained to accurately detect factors that affect their decisions, and (2) whether RL can directly opti-mize for the disclosure of these factors thereby improving LLM self-explanations’ faithfulness. We experiment with two intervention types: random-word insertions and user-bias insertions, using a per-sample reward derived from the Phi-CCT correlation metric. RL fine-tuned Llama3.1-8B and Qwen3-8B show substantial improvements on the Phi-CCT faithfulness metric, with in-distribution scores ris-ing from near-zero to as high as 0.664, and out-of-distribution scores reaching up to 0.691 on held-out tasks such as StrategyQA. Cross-intervention generalization is weaker but more interesting: a priori we would not expect a model trained only on random word insertions to generalize to user-bias phrases, yet Llama3.1-8B shows non-zero transfer in this direction. The reverse direction and Qwen3-8B do not replicate this, indicating model-dependent and setup-dependent effects we cannot yet explain. Lastly we analyze model behavior to rule out reward gaming behaviors that often plague RL training. Ultimately, we show that models can be trained to implicitly identify influential factors and disclose them, offering a scalable path toward reducing unfaithful reasoning in LLMs. 

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

The faithfulness of a model’s self-explanation is the degree to which its reasoning traces re-flect its internal decision-making (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020). As models become more capable, the plausibility of their reasoning may improve, yet faithfulness does not necessarily follow; instead, models often generate convincing justifications for decisions actually driven by hidden biases or shortcuts (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Arcuschin et al., 2026). This distinction between plausibility and faithfulness undermines the use of reasoning traces for model oversight (Agarwal et al., 2024). When justifications are decoupled from decision-making, the explanations become a fabricated paper trail, which may not reflect the true decision-making process, complicating safety

This highlights a critical failure in current paradigms that prioritize output quality over pro-cess transparency (Perez et al., 2022), necessitating training frameworks for optimizing faithfulness. 

Current literature broadly categorizes self explanations (also referred thereafter as explanation or rationale) faithfulness into two types: explanatory faithfulness and causal faithfulness (Siegel et al., 2024). Explanatory faithfulness investigates whether an explanation reflects the decision-making process of the model (Atanasova et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023; Siegel et al., 2024; 2025). Causal faithfulness investigates whether the model’s prediction causally depends on the generated rationale (Turpin et al., 2023; Radhakrishnan et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2025). In this paper, we focus on explanatory faithfulness, and follow the prevailing methodology within the explanatory faithfulness literature by employing counterfactual tests (Pearl, 2009; Atanasova et al., 2023; Turpin et al., 2023; Siegel et al., 2024; 2025). Counterfactual tests involve creating pairs of factual and counterfactual inputs to examine how model decisions and explanations shift under controlled interventions, such as the introduction of biasing hints. This approach allows us to identify the factors that functionally drive a model’s output and verify whether the model’s self explanations accurately disclose those factors. While evaluation frameworks have matured by using counterfactual interventions to detect when explanations diverge from internal mechanics (Atanasova et al., 2023; Siegel et al., 2025; 2024), methods to improve faithfulness remain limited. Inference-time strategies, such as decomposing problems or using logic programs (Radhakrishnan et al., 2023; Arakelyan et al.), improve tractability but do not alter the model’s parameters or internal representations. Conversely, existing training frameworks often align decisions and explanations with external ‘gold’ labels rather than ensuring internal consistency between the model’s reasoning and its outputs (Paul et al.; Ramnath et al., 2023). Training methods have been applied to related faithfulness goals-FRODO (Paul et al.) uses DPO to enforce causal dependency between rationale and answer, and MARIO (Ramnath et al., 2023) uses RL with a consistency reward against external gold labels-but neither directly optimizes for ex-planatory faithfulness in the sense captured by counterfactual correlation metrics such as Phi-CCT (Siegel et al., 2025) and CCT (Siegel et al., 2024). This is a genuinely harder target to optimize: faithfulness in this sense has no static ground truth, since whether an explanation is faithful depends on the specific model’s own decisions and how they change under intervention, so preference pairs or ’gold labels’ cannot be pre-computed once and reused across models or training steps. Reinforce-ment Learning is a natural fit for objectives of this form–where the reward must be computed from the model’s own behavior at each step (Shao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2024)-and to our knowledge has not yet been applied to explanatory faithfulness. 

Our method and contributions. To bridge this gap we present a novel framework for improving an LLM’s self explanation’s faithfulness by incorporating signals derived from counterfactuals in Siegel et al. (2025) and Turpin et al. (2023) into a reward that can be used by RL algorithms such as GRPO (Shao et al., 2024). The model is rewarded for producing explanations that mention factors identified as influential while omitting factors that have been non-influential to its decision. We find that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) significantly improves a model’s ability to predict influ-ence, raising correlation scores between predicted and actual influence from near-zero to over 0.66. However, this does not translate to the model’s ability to verbalize these influential factors. Even when explicitly prompted to explain their decisions, SFT models struggle to verbalize the factors they have correctly identified as influential. In contrast, our RL method successfully teaches models to both detect and faithfully disclose in-fluential factors, matching introspection performance with Phi-CCT score of up to 0.664 - a metric measuring the correlation between a model’s internal decision drivers and its verbalized reason-ing. Crucially, this capability is not merely memorized, as it generalizes effectively to held-out datasets (reaching 0.691 Phi-CCT) and encourages concise, principled reasoning without resorting to reward-hacking behaviors such as degenerate copying. 2To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align) 

## 2 RELATED WORK 

Faithfulness, the extent to which explanations accurately reflect a models internal reasoning, has long been distinguished from plausibility and truthfulness (Jacovi & Goldberg, 2020; Atanasova et al., 2023). Whereas plausible explanations may simply sound correct, faithful ones reveal the actual causal factors behind predictions, a distinction critical in safety-sensitive domains such as healthcare, law, and security (Agarwal et al., 2024; Wood-Doughty et al., 2021). Empirical studies consistently show that LLMs often fail this test: chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning is not reliably used in decision-making (Lanham et al., 2023; Paul et al.), explanations omit influential features (Turpin et al., 2023), and bias-inducing prompt variations shift predictions without acknowledgment in rationales (Chua et al., 2025). 

Evaluation methods increasingly frame faithfulness in terms of counterfactual model behavior. Atanasova et al. (2023) introduced interventions to test whether explanations mention features that alter predictions. Siegel et al. (2024; 2025) refined this into a correlational measure, aligning men-tions with the graded influence of interventions. Lanham et al. (2023) perturb reasoning traces directly, e.g. ablation or mistake insertion in CoT to test whether answers depend on the stated reasoning. Parcalabescu & Frank (2024) highlight that many faithfulness metrics collapse to output self-consistency rather than mechanism-level dependence. 

Frameworks to improve faithfulness have been proposed to improve causal faithfulness through ensuring the contents of LLM’s CoT reasoning drives their final predictions. Radhakrishnan et al. (2023) proposed a prompting framework that decomposes problems before making a decision. This guides model reasoning into structured sub-questions, thereby improving the verifiability of the model’s decision-making process. FLARE (Arakelyan et al.) prompts models to formalize prob-lems into Prolog logic programs, which are simulated by the LLM to enable "soft reasoning" with symbolic backtracking. Comparing these simulated traces to actual executions allows for the detec-tion of hallucinations and reasoning failures. However, both methods are inference-time strategies which improves faithfulness without fundamentally altering the model’s underlying parameters. 

Training frameworks embed faithfulness constraints directly into model parameters. Paul et al. introduce FRODO, which decouples the process into two components: an inference module and a reasoning module. The inference module uses Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) to train the model to prefer valid reasoning chains over intentionally erroneous or irrelevant counterfactual ones. Meanwhile, the reasoning module is optimized via a multi-objective loss that explicitly enforces a causal dependency between the rationale and the answer. Similarly, Chua et al. (2025) use supervised fine-tuning to de-bias models; by removing latent biased features, they reduce the presence of hidden factors, thereby indirectly improving the faithfulness of self-disclosed rationales. Ramnath et al. (2023) propose MARIO, which utilizes a consistency reward (via QUARK (Lu et al., 2022)) to generate rationales that maximize an external judge’s likelihood of predicting the ground-truth label. We argue this objective diverges from explanatory faithfulness; an external judge eval-uates whether a rationale logically supports a "gold" answer as judged by an external model, pri-oritizing plausibility over true introspection. Unlike MARIO, which optimizes for persuasive jus-tifications that may suppress the disclosure of internal biases, our approach relies on observable influence as a proxy for the model’s internal computations, ensuring explanations reflect actual de-cision drivers rather than external expectations. 

Reinforcement learning in LLMs has been prevalent since Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) has been used to align models with human preferences (Ouyang et al., 2022; Christiano et al., 2023). While McMillan et al. (2025) show RLHF can improve faithfulness in specific settings, it does not explicitly target faithfulness metrics. Shao et al. (2024) popularized the use of Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to optimize LLMs for mathematical reasoning, which has since been extended to tasks such as commonsense and scientific reasoning (Liu et al., 2024). Other RL algorithms have also been proposed to improve reasoning in LLMs using verifiable rewards from an environment such as a code interpreter, such as MAGISTRAL (Rastogi et al., 2025), OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025), and Seed-15 (Seed et al., 2025). Unlike tasks with external ground truth, faithfulness depends on the model’s internal decision pathways. We must therefore 3To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align) evaluate it by probing model behavior to verify if the explanation aligns with the causal drivers of the decision. 

## 3 METHOD 

Without access to a model’s internal computations corresponding to its self explanation process, we cannot directly determine whether any certain factor (e.g., a token or phrase in the prompt) causally affected the decision. Instead, we rely on observable influence as a proxy for internal computation. Given a model with parameters θ that takes a prompt X and produces a decision Y and an explana-tion Z, there exist latent factors affecting the decision and explanation as U , which includes dataset distribution, model tendencies, decoding noise, etc.: 

Y = fθ (X, U Y )

Z = gθ (X, Y, U Z ) (1) As shown in Figure 1a, we are typically not privy to the latent factors U that drive a model’s decision. This creates a risk of unfaithfulness: the explanation Z may be shaped by U rather than the prompt logic, so a model might rely on a latent bias while producing a plausible-sounding justification that ignores the true driver (the U → Z shortcut). Consider a Social-IQA-style prompt X: "Jordan stayed late at the office to finish a colleague’s report. Why did Jordan do this?" with options (A) to be helpful and (B) to take credit. The model predicts Y = A, explaining that staying late reads as generous - but factors that drove the model to the conclusion is unknown, and that is fine. To evaluate the ability of a model to disclose its influential factors, we apply a single-factor inter-vention ∆ to X to produce X′ = X ⊕ ∆. We do not need to identify U ; what matters is that ∆

is the only controlled difference between X and X′, so any change in behavior can be attributed to it. As illustrated in Figure 1b, this opens a measurable causal path ∆ → X′ → Y ′ and lets us test whether Z′ reflects it. Inserting (∆ = secretly ) yields X′: "Jordan secretly stayed late at the office to finish a colleague’s report," and suppose the model now predicts Y ′ = B. The flip is attributable to ∆, and a faithful Z′ should disclose it - e.g., " secretly suggests Jordan concealed the act, which is more consistent with taking credit." If instead Z′ restates the "generous colleague" justification while quietly outputting B, the explanation is a paper trail decoupled from the actual driver. This gives an operational definition of faithfulness: alignment between whether ∆ changed the decision ( Y̸ = Y ′) and whether ∆ is mentioned in Z′. On its own, this alignment provides a noisy per-example training signal usable within a minibatch; aggregated across a dataset, it becomes the Phi-CCT metric, formalized next.

on Y ′.3.1 FAITHFULNESS OF SELF -EXPLANATION : DEFINITION AND EVALUATION 

The faithfulness of an LLM’s self-explanation evaluates whether the self-explanation mentions the true causal factors underlying the LLM’s decisions. Leveraging the causal path X′ → Y ′, we identify ∆ as a necessary factor only if its insertion alters the outcome decision Y ′. We define influence I as whether the intervention changes the decision, I = 1{Y ′̸ = Y }, and mention M as whether the explanation cites the intervention, M = 1{∆ mentioned in Z′}.

This is a proxy for Phi-CCT faithfulness metric (Siegel et al., 2025) defined as the correlation between influence and mention over an entire dataset: Phi-CCT = Corr( I, M). For our reward in RL training, we use a per-sample binary indicator of whether influence and mention agree. Note this is a training signal distinct from Phi-CCT itself, which is a dataset-level correlation: 

r = 1{M ⇐⇒ I} .

If we consider influence as a binary indicator, each factual-counterfactual pair falls into one of four cases: Influential and Mentioned (TP – true positive), Influential and Not Mentioned (FN – false negative), Non-Influential and Mentioned (FP), Non-Influential and Not Mentioned (TN). Tests proposed in Atanasova et al. (2023) and Chen et al. (2025) only consider of the influential cases (TP and FN), if the token is mentioned. Phi-CCT (Siegel et al., 2025) and CCT (Siegel et al., 2024) consider all four cases by calculating a correlation score over the entire dataset’s influential features and mentions. Thus our reward formulation captures both sides of the influence-mention relationship, and penalizes indiscriminate mentioning (mentions every token regardless of influence) or indiscriminate influence (always be influenced) and thus provide more robust training signals without exposing trivial strategies for reward hacking. 3.2 REINFORCEMENT LEARNING FRAMEWORK FOR IMPROVING FAITHFULNESS OF SELF -EXPLANATIONS 

To optimize the faithfulness of an LLM’s self-explanations, we propose a reinforcement learning framework that integrates counterfactual evaluation into the training loop (Figure 2). At each train-ing step, we sample counterfactual prompts from the dataset, elicit model decisions and explana-tions, and compute influence and mention labels to derive faithfulness rewards. The model is up-dated using Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) (Shao et al., 2024), which normalizes advantages within groups to stabilize training. 5To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align) 3.3 OUR RESEARCH HYPOTHESES 

Our study comprises three hypotheses: (1) Influence Introspection: With supervised fine-tuning, models can detect which interventions flip their own decisions. We fine-tune using binary Yes/No 

labels for I and measure correlation between predicted and true influence of factors. (2) Influence-Mention Alignment: Using RL, we reward the LLM for M = I. This encourages the model to mention influential factors and suppress irrelevant ones. (3) Generalization: We test whether this learned behavior transfers to OOD data and intervention types (random insertions vs. user-bias edits). 3.4 DATA SPLITS AND DOMAINS 

To evaluate both robust learning and generalization, we partition our data into Train, Test, and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) splits. The Train and Test datasets are comprised of e-SNLI (Camburu et al., 2018) and Social-IQA (Sap et al., 2019). The OOD split is comprised of ComVE (Wang et al., 2020) and StrategyQA (Geva et al., 2021), used exclusively to test the transferability of the model’s abilities. 3.5 EVALUATION METRICS FOR FAITHFULNESS AND GENERALIZABILITY 

We evaluate influence introspection via predicted vs true label correlation over the entire dataset. We evaluate influence-mention alignment via correlation over the entire dataset i.e. Phi-CCT scores. For all metrics, we compute 95% confidence intervals using a percentile bootstrap with 5,000 re-samples, each drawn with replacement from the available dataset (approximately 2,000 examples per split). 3.6 METRICS FOR POTENTIAL REWARD GAMING 

Phi-CCT scores on a balanced dataset is robust to the simplest static reward-hacking strategies: indiscriminate mentioning (always outputting the intervention) and collapse to silence (never men-tioning it) both yield a correlation of zero. However, high performance metrics do not guarantee explanation quality. We hypothesize that optimization pressure can induce degenerate behaviors (hacks) that satisfy the binary reward condition. 

Potential hack 1: Risk-Averse Disclosure To minimize the risk of penalization when misidenti-fying and therefore disclosing a non-influential token, the model may learn to shorten its output in general. We monitor Completion Length , defined as the word count of the counterfactual explana-tion Z’, for systematic disparities between predicted positives and negatives which would indicate a shortcut strategy rather than consistent reasoning.

## 4 EXPERIMENTS 

To test our hypotheses, we conduct experiments across two LLMs: Qwen3-8B and Llama3.1-8B. Base models and trained variants of the model on Random Insertions and User-Bias interventions are evaluated (details below). Trained models are fine-tuned on the Influence Introspection via Su-pervised Fine-Tuning or Influence-Mention Alignment via Reinforcement Learning, then evaluated on both faithfulness tests and all datasets to assess generalization. 6To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align) 4.1 DATASET PREPARATION 

Dataset construction. Faithfulness is not a property of a dataset; it is a relationship between a specific model’s internal reasoning and its output hence an "influential" intervention for Model A might be "mere noise" for Model B. Therefore we need to construct separate faithfulness datasets per model x dataset permutation. First we enrich existing questions in each dataset with counterfactual variants. For every factual prompt x, an intervention ∆ is inserted to create a counterfactual prompt 

x′ = x ⊕ ∆. ⊕ here denotes an insertion operation - in practice we generate multiple candidate interventions and perform rejection sampling by using an LLM to rank the interventions in terms of natural and semantic cohesion, and only keeping the best counterfactual prompt and intervention among all candidates of this specific prompt. We then obtain factual and counterfactual behaviors under deterministic decoding ( T = 0 ): the factual decision and explanation (y, z ) are generated from x, and the counterfactual decision and explanation (y′, z ′) from x′. Each data instance is thus represented as a tuple (x, x ′, ∆, y, z, y ′, z ′) from which faithfulness metrics can be computed and training datasets derived. Data is split into Train, Test, and Out-of-Distribution (OOD) sets to test cross-domain transfer. Two types of interventions are applied: (i) Random Insertions, where neutral phrases are randomly inserted into the prompt. What words can be inserted and where to insert them is stochastic within constraints - we use NLTK and POS-tagging to identify insertion slots (e.g., placing an adverb before a verb). This ensures the structural integrity of the sentence remains intact, and the rejection sampling step mentioned above retains the best prompt by filtering out candidates that are syntactically correct but semantically nonsensical (e.g., "The man is transparently walking over the steps"). (ii) User-Bias Interventions, where biased hints are added to nudge the model towards a specific answer (e.g., "My teacher believes the answer is A"). Lastly specific instructions are then added at the end of the prompts to elicit self-explanations or introspective answers with a certain format suitable for parsing. Details in A.1. 

Dataset Balance. We ensure train, test and OOD datasets have balanced classes of influential samples ( I = 1 ) and non-influential samples ( I = 0 ). This is to prevent reward hacking behavior: for example if the dataset’s contains mostly ( I = 0 ) samples, the model can maximize rewards by indiscriminately outputting total silence and therefore any ∆ is never mentioned. To achieve parity we iteratively sample interventions until the minority class reaches n/ 2 instances, then perform random subsampling without replacement from the majority class to match this count. By ensuring that there is no optimization pressure to prefer one class over another, we force the RL agent to distinguish between cases where the intervention was a causal driver and cases where it was mere structural noise. Balancing datasets ensures that a high Phi-CCT score (correlation) truly reflects the model’s introspective capability, as any fixed strategy (e.g., "always output the intervention token") would result in a reward mean of 0.5 and a correlation of zero. Examples of dataset can be found in Appendix B. 4.2 TRAINING SETUP 

Influence Introspection training via Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). We train a model to recog-nize when an intervention alters its own decision. Given a factual-counterfactual pair (x, x ′, ∆) , can the model learn to predict whether the inserted intervention ∆ changes its decisions I = 1{y′̸ = y}.The model is then fine-tuned to output ’Yes’ or ’No’ according to the true influence label, optimized with cross-entropy loss. The model is then evaluated by measuring correlation between predicted and true influence labels over the entire dataset. 

Influence-Mention Alignment via Reinforcement Learning. We train a model to disclose an influential token only if it causally influences its decision. Each training example consists of a factual-counterfactual tuple (x, y, z, ∆, x ′, y ′, z ′). At each training step, we use factual decisions 

y collected from this specific model during dataset preparation and generate counterfactual deci-sions y′ in separate contexts, and use the difference to compute influence I = 1{y′̸ = y}. In a new context, we append the counterfactual decision y′ to the counterfactual prompt x′ and generate counterfactual explanations z′. We then detect whether the intervention ∆ is mentioned ( M) and compute the faithfulness reward r = 1{M ⇐⇒ I} . The reward is used to calculate loss with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) on only the counterfactual explanations generated -hence encouraging the model’s explanations to include influential factors in its decisions, and omit non-influential factors. Off-policy nature of the training setup. Influence labels I are computed once from the frozen initial model and not resampled during training. As the policy shifts, these labels may become stale: a prompt labelled "influential" at T0 may no longer flip the current model’s decision, and vice versa. Faithfulness is defined relative to the acting model’s decisions, so training reward and evaluation metric are measuring subtly different objects–the reward targets alignment with T0’s decisions, while evaluation targets alignment with the trained model’s current decisions. Reported Phi-CCT gains may therefore partly reflect the trained model’s decisions drifting toward the stale labels, rather than purely improved disclosure. A fully on-policy variant that resamples I each step is the natural next step, and we flag this as an important open question for follow-up work. 

## 5 RESULTS 

Influence Introspection Test. SFT markedly improves models’ ability to detect influential inter-ventions within distribution. On random insertions, Llama3.1-8B rises from -0.013 to 0.537 (test-set Phi-CCT), and Qwen3-8B from 0.110 to 0.552. On user-bias interventions, Llama3.1-8B improves from 0.036 to 0.664 and Qwen3-8B from 0.032 to 0.607. Confusion matrices show higher true-positive rates and reduced false negatives. However, this does not transfer across intervention types or to OOD datasets, except modest generalization for user-bias detection (up to 0.691 Phi-CCT on Qwen3-8B). When evaluated on the RL task, SFT-trained models fail to verbalize detected influ-ences, often providing generic explanations (the word does not affect my decision). Thus, SFT teaches detection but not disclosure. 

Influence-Mention alignment. RL with a counterfactual reward aligns explanations with causal influence. For random insertions, Phi-CCT increases to 0.536 (Llama3.1-8B) and 0.404 (Qwen3-8B); for user bias, to 0.664 and 0.607 respectively. Base models systematically under-disclose bias influences, but RL substantially increases true positives. RL-trained models generalize better than SFT to OOD sets (up to 0.691 Phi-CCT), likely because training operates in natural-language space. Cross-intervention transfer remains limited, though small gains appear when Llama3.1-8B trained on random insertions is tested on user bias (0.178 Phi-CCT). RL also partially transfers to introspec-tion, modestly improving correlation on SFT tasks. This shows that RL can teach models to both detect and faithfully disclose influential factors, im-proving the faithfulness of LLM self-explanations. The reverse direction does not produce the same effect, and Qwen3-8B does not replicate the finding, pointing to model-family effects we cannot yet explain. 5.1 FURTHER ANALYSIS OF MODEL BEHAVIOR 

To ensure improvements are genuine and not the result of reward hacking, we analyze two behavioral metrics: Completion Length and Overlap Ratio as formally defined in Sec. 3.5. 

Risk-Averse Disclosure (Shortening Completion Length) We observe that RL training leads to systematically shorter explanations (Figure 4a), particularly for predicted negatives (True Negatives and False Negatives). This indicates the model adopts a "risk-averse" strategy: it becomes concise when it detects no influence, thereby minimizing the chance of hallucinating a mention (False Posi-tive). Explanations for user-bias interventions remain longer than random insertions, suggesting the model might internalize that overriding a bias requires more "reasoning effort" or justification than ignoring a random word. Since baseline models rarely verbalize user-bias influence, RL training helps bridge this gap, encouraging models to both recognize and articulate the impact of user-bias interventions. 

Absence of Conditional Parroting (Overlap Ratio) A potential failure mode is parroting parts of the prompt to guarantee a mention match. We find no significant increase in the Overlap Ratio 9To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align) (Figure 4b) for True Positive cases after RL training, indicating improvement in faithfulness scores is driven by selective integration of the intervention into the reasoning chain, not by verbatim copying. 5.2 SAFETY AND INTERPRETABILITY IMPLICATIONS 

Optimizing for faithful disclosure introduces critical safety trade-offs. Our results show RL training for faithful models produce shorter explanations to avoid inaccurate disclosure, which increases pre-cision but may reduce interpretability by omitting the broader context required for human oversight. Conversely, the model’s learned ability to distinguish between "random noise" and "user biases" suggests a path toward adversarial robustness, potentially making models less susceptible to jail-breaks or prompt injections that rely on hidden influential tokens. Finally, we note that while RL effectively optimizes for disclosure, the high-pressure training signal may affect model calibration. 

## 6 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

We investigated whether Large Language Models can be trained to generate faithful self-explanations. Our results demonstrate a clear dichotomy between detection and disclosure: (1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) effectively teaches models to introspect (detect influence) but fails to enforce the verbalization of that influence. (2) Reinforcement Learning (RL) with counterfactual rewards successfully aligns explanations with decision mechanics. It encourages models to disclose influential factors and omit irrelevant ones. Crucially, the RL approach generalizes well to OOD datasets and avoids simple reward-hacking strategies like parroting. This offers a scalable path to-ward models that not only reason correctly but transparently disclose the actual drivers of their deci-sions. Future work includes the development of a fully on-policy framework that integrates balanced counterfactual sampling directly into the training loop, ensuring the reward signal evolves alongside the model’s shifting policy. Future work can investigate incorporation of multi-objective rewards to maintain task performance; however, we recognize that this increases optimization pressure on the predicted labels, potentially heightening the risk of training instability or reward hacking. We view training-time faithfulness objectives as a promising component of a broader oversight stack, com-plementing evaluation-time faithfulness tests and interpretability methods, rather than a standalone guarantee of transparent reasoning. 

## 7 LIMITATIONS 

Quantification of performance and behavior before and after training. We train and evaluate faithfulness with respect to the original model’s decisions y, y ′, which were collected prior to train-ing. As the model’s policy shifts during training, these pre-computed decisions may drift from the current model’s behavior. We do not quantify this drift, nor the corresponding change in task per-formance, and we lack qualitative analysis of how reasoning style changes before and after training. Our evaluation also omits plausibility and human-preference scores for the generated explanations. Finally, SFT training may theoretically encourage premature <eos> outputs that reduce explanation completeness; we neither confirmed nor ruled out this failure mode. 

Intervention insertion and mention detection. To detect mentions, we match morphological variants of the intervention word, which may miss synonyms or paraphrased references to the same concept and therefore underestimate mention rates. Our random insertions are restricted to single words, which is a simplification, and despite rejection sampling by naturalness ranking we cannot guarantee that intervened sentences are free of detectable semantic shifts. 

Dataset scope, model scale, and model family. Experiments use balanced 6k-sample subsets of four datasets, which covers a small slice of natural language tasks. Model scale is capped at 8B to fit single-GPU training, and only Qwen3 and Llama3.1 were tested; we did not evaluate larger models, different model families, or instruction-tuned variants beyond these two. 10 To appear at the ICLR 2026 Workshop on Representational Alignment (Re-Align)
