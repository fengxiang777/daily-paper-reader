Abstract 

Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) is a prominent parameter-efficient fine-tuning method for large language models, yet its effectiveness is critically shaped by how a fixed rank budget is allocated across Transformer modules. Existing adaptive-rank LoRA variants typically reallocate ranks using training-time local gradient statis-tics, incurring additional memory and computational overhead. However, this local sensitivity-driven view leaves the role of task-conditioned global information-flow structure in rank allocation largely underexplored. To address this gap, we propose IFCLoRA (Information-Flow Centrality LoRA), a topology-aware, pre-fine-tuning rank allocation procedure. IFCLoRA performs lightweight tracing on a frozen pretrained model using a small calibration set, constructing a task-conditioned sparse interaction graph whose nodes correspond to LoRA-insertable modules. The method then combines a global information-flow topology prior with node-wise local gradient sensitivity to compute Information-Flow Centrality (IFC) scores, which quantify each node’s adaptation importance under task-conditioned multi-hop propagation. Finally, IFCLoRA assigns discrete ranks in a single shot under a global rank constraint. Across models and tasks, under the same training setup and total rank budgets as representative baselines such as LoRA, AdaLoRA, and EVA, IFCLoRA consistently improves aggregate performance in multiple low-rank regimes while incurring training-time costs comparable to standard LoRA. For example, when fine-tuning LLaMA3-8B for mathematical reasoning, IFCLoRA im-proves over LoRA by 1.36% and 1.82% at rank 4 and rank 8, respectively. Further analyses reveal task-dependent, non-uniform allocation patterns in the learned rank profiles, providing an interpretable view of how adaptation capacity is distributed

1 Introduction 

Large pretrained language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong generalization ability across a wide range of natural language processing and reasoning tasks [Vaswani et al., 2017, Grattafiori et al., 2024, Yang et al., 2025]. However, as model sizes continue to grow, full fine-tuning faces prohibitively high memory and storage costs. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) has accordingly emerged as a practical paradigm for adapting large models with limited trainable parameters [Han et al., 2024]. Among PEFT methods, LoRA parameterizes weight updates as low-rank matrix products, substantially reducing the number of trainable parameters while maintaining competitive performance [Hu et al., 2022]. Standard LoRA assigns the same rank to all target layers and modules, implicitly assuming that different positions contribute similarly to the task. In low-budget settings, however, Transformer layers and modules exhibit substantial functional heterogeneity. Prior analyses of Transformer circuits and feed-forward memories further suggest that attention heads, FFN blocks, and residual pathways play distinct roles in information processing [Geva et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022, Meng et al., 2022, Syed et al., 2024]. These components differ in how they store, route, and transform information. In low-budget regimes, model performance can be sensitive to whether limited capacity is concentrated on task-relevant information-propagation paths or spread uniformly. Although adaptive rank-allocation methods improve over uniform allocation [Zhang et al., 2023, He et al., 2025, Ed-dib et al., 2025], they still mainly rely on local statistics during training and introduce additional overhead. A natural question is therefore whether a global perspective that explicitly characterizes cross-layer information flow can provide a better allocation signal. Mechanistic interpretability reveals traceable task-relevant computational subgraphs [Conmy et al., 2023, Syed et al., 2024]. Graph centrality analysis provides tools for quantifying global node importance [Page et al., 1999, Newman, 2018]. 2 Related Work 

2.1 PEFT and Adaptive Rank Allocation 

Representative PEFT methods include Adapter Tuning [Houlsby et al., 2019], Prefix Tuning [Li and Liang, 2021], Prompt Tuning [Lester et al., 2021], BitFit [Ben Zaken et al., 2022], IA3 [Liu et al., 2022], and LoRA [Hu et al., 2022]. LoRA parameterizes the weight increment as a low-rank decomposition, W = W(0) + BA , where B ∈ Rd1×r and A ∈ Rr×d2 with r ≪ { d1, d 2}. It greatly reduces trainable parameters and memory overhead, making it one of the most widely used PEFT baselines. The uniform rank allocation adopted by standard LoRA fails to account for the pervasive functional heterogeneity across layers and modules in Transformers. Recent work has therefore explored adaptive rank allocation from several directions: AdaLoRA and ALoRA estimate rank importance during optimization or under a fixed adaptation budget and redistribute capacity accordingly, EVA and TLoRA use downstream activations, variance, task-aware pre-fine-tuning signals, or sensitivity cues, GeLoRA and GoRA use representation geometry or gradient information, and IGU-LoRA and L1RA use attribution, uncertainty, or dynamic rank-assignment mechanisms [Zhang et al., 2023, Liu et al., 2024, Paischer et al., 2025, Lin et al., 2026, Ed-dib et al., 2025, He et al., 2025, Cui et al., 2026, Sundararajan et al., 2017, Singh et al., 2025, Deng et al., 2026]. These methods are closely related to IFCLoRA in their goal of avoiding uniform rank allocation, but their allocation signals are still primarily derived from local matrix-level, activation-level, gradient-level, attribution-level, geometric, dynamic-assignment, or training-time statistics. In contrast, IFCLoRA explicitly constructs a task-conditioned interaction graph and converts global source-to-sink information-flow topology into a pre-fine-tuning, one-shot rank-allocation prior, lightly calibrated by local gradient sensitivity. 

2.2 Mechanistic Interpretability and Circuit Analysis 

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to understand how neural networks realize specific functions by examining internal computational structure. Prior work suggests that language models contain traceable task-relevant computational subgraphs: attention heads, MLP layers, and residual pathways can jointly form circuits that implement specific functions [Elhage et al., 2021, Wang et al., 2022, Conmy et al., 2023, Syed et al., 2024, Hadad et al., 2026], while causal tracing and model editing locate localized computations that mediate factual recall [Meng et al., 2022]. Studies of component roles further reveal the memory-like function of feed-forward networks [Geva et al., 2021]. Although these methods reveal important structural regularities, they mostly belong to the post hoc analysis paradigm. The resulting graph structures have not yet been systematically used to guide parameter allocation before fine-tuning, and full circuit extraction is often computationally expensive. IFCLoRA borrows the idea of modeling internal interactions as a directed graph, but redirects it from post hoc explanation to pre-fine-tuning rank allocation.

3 Method 

We propose IFCLoRA, a topology-aware rank allocation method for low-budget PEFT. Unlike LoRA and training-time dynamic methods such as AdaLoRA [Hu et al., 2022, Zhang et al., 2023], IFCLoRA performs lightweight circuit tracing with a small calibration set before fine-tuning begins, constructs a task-conditioned interaction graph, and completes rank allocation in one shot. The method consists of four stages: task-conditioned interaction graph construction, topology-prior extraction, local sensitivity calibration, and budget-constrained rank allocation. Figure 2 illustrates the complete IFCLoRA workflow from calibration-based tracing to budget-constrained rank routing. 43.1 Problem Definition 

Let the pretrained Transformer be M, and let U = {u1, . . . , u U } denote all LoRA-insertable modules, also referred to as adaptation units, such as attention projection layers and MLP projection layers. Our goal is to assign an integer rank ru to each unit u under a total rank budget Rtot , yielding a rank allocation vector r = ( r1, . . . , r U ) ∈ ZU . The budget controlled in this paper is the total rank budget rather than a strictly matched total number of trainable parameters, so small parameter-count differences may remain when modules have different shapes. Thus, IFCLoRA focuses on how different allocation strategies affect the utilization efficiency of limited adaptation capacity under the same rank budget: UX

Standard LoRA assumes that different layers and modules have similar adaptation requirements and therefore adopts a uniform rank allocation. In contrast, we argue that low-budget performance depends on whether limited capacity is allocated to modules that truly lie on key reasoning paths. The core problem is therefore how to estimate the relative task importance of LoRA-insertable modules before training and use that estimate for one-shot rank allocation. To solve this problem, IFCLoRA first constructs a task-conditioned interaction graph from a small calibration set. It then extracts a topology prior, calibrates it with local task sensitivity, and finally maps the resulting IFC score to a discrete rank allocation under the total budget constraint. 

3.2 Task-Conditioned Interaction Graph Construction 

We represent interactions among LoRA-insertable modules as a task-conditioned sparse directed interaction graph G = ( V, E ). Each node vi ∈ V corresponds to a LoRA-insertable module, also referred to as an adaptation unit, and each directed edge (i → j) ∈ E indicates that a change in node 

i has an observable influence on a later node j under the current task. The goal is not to recover the full computation circuit exactly, but to build a sparse and tractable structural approximation that carries task-relevant information flow. (b) Layer-wise average allocated rank across allocation strategies. 8Figure 4(a) shows that IFCLoRA learns a clearly non-uniform rank allocation. More capacity is assigned to mid-to-late FFN modules and selected attention projections, while shallow modules are compressed to lower ranks. Similar non-uniform rank profiles have also been observed in recent adaptive-rank LoRA studies, whereas IFCLoRA further links the allocation pattern to task-conditioned information-flow topology [Ed-dib et al., 2025, He et al., 2025, Singh et al., 2025]. This allocation is consistent with prior observations that Transformer FFN layers can act as key-value memories, and suggests that mid-to-late FFN modules may occupy more source-to-sink information-flow paths under the GSM8K calibration distribution [Geva et al., 2021]. In this sense, the learned rank allocation is not merely non-uniform, but aligned with the topology prior used by IFC. The layer-wise view further compares the average allocated rank across strategies. Figure 4(b) shows that LoRA uses a flat allocation, while AdaLoRA produces a more dispersed dynamic allocation pattern across layers. In contrast, IFCLoRA concentrates rank around a smaller set of layers that are structurally important under the task-conditioned interaction graph. EVA is included as a data-driven initialization and rank-redistribution baseline in the quantitative comparisons in Table 1 and Appendix D; this subsection focuses on the recorded rank-allocation visualizations for LoRA, AdaLoRA, and IFCLoRA. These observations suggest that low-budget adaptation can benefit from routing capacity toward key modules rather than distributing it uniformly or relying only on diffuse dynamic reallocation. 

4.4 Ablation Study 

Figure 5: Ablation study and routing-control baselines on Qwen3-8B (GSM8K, r = 4 ). We ablate the routing score on Qwen3-8B (GSM8K, r = 4 ), covering routing-control base-lines (Random, Depth-biased, and FFN-biased), PageRank, graph-construction ablation (w/o zero ablation), component ablations (w/o Gradient and w/o Topology), and the full IFCLoRA score. As shown in Figure 5, IFCLoRA achieves the best accuracy (82.33), with w/o Gradient being the strongest ablation (81.88), while w/o zero ablation matches FFN-biased (81.56) and remains below the topology-based variants. The simple routing-control baselines improve over LoRA (80.97) but lag behind w/o Gradient, suggesting that the task-conditioned interaction graph captures allocation signals beyond fixed depth, module type, random assignment, or generic graph centrality. The low w/o Topology result (81.13) further suggests that local gradient-only routing may miss structurally important propagation paths. Overall, these results support the complementarity of global topology, causal zero-ablation tracing, and local task sensitivity. A broader sensitivity analysis over ρ, τ , and calibration size is provided in Appendix C. 

5 Discussion 

Topology-aware rank allocation offers a structural perspective on PEFT. Instead of treating LoRA rank as a uniform hyperparameter or adapting it only from local training-time statistics, IFCLoRA views LoRA-insertable modules as nodes in a task-conditioned interaction graph. This reframes low-budget adaptation as routing limited capacity along task-relevant information-flow paths. The resulting non-uniform rank profiles suggest that useful adaptation capacity is not evenly distributed across Transformer modules, but is shaped by the global organization of task-specific computation. Information-Flow Centrality is the key mechanism that operationalizes this perspective. By combining source-to-sink reachability with local gradient sensitivity, IFC estimates whether a module both receives task-relevant signals and lies on influential propagation paths. This makes the learned rank allocation more interpretable than purely local criteria: the assigned ranks can be traced back to graph structure and task-conditioned perturbation responses. We emphasize, however, that this provides structural evidence for capacity routing rather than a complete causal explanation of the model’s internal mechanism. IFCLoRA has several limitations. The IFC score is a heuristic without formal 9optimality guarantees: there is no theoretical result establishing that the resulting allocation minimizes downstream task loss under a given budget. The graph construction also assumes a forward-causal node ordering specific to decoder-only Transformers, and whether the approach transfers to encoder-decoder or mixture-of-experts architectures remains an open question. Finally, our experiments cover GSM8K and selected SuperGLUE tasks on three backbones, leaving broader domains and larger scales to be validated. 

6 Conclusion and Future Work 

We presented IFCLoRA, a topology-aware one-shot rank allocation method for LoRA fine-tuning. The method preserves a standard LoRA-like training stage while replacing uniform capacity assignment with a task-conditioned structural prior. Future work includes cheaper graph construction, adaptive recalibration, stronger causal interventions for validating IFC-identified bottlenecks, and extensions to code generation, long-context reasoning, instruction tuning, multimodal adaptation, and broader PEFT design choices such as insertion locations and module types.

LoRA is the non-adaptive baseline, AdaLoRA is the training-time dynamic allocation baseline, EVA is the activation-variance initialization and redistribution baseline, and IFCLoRA computes its rank allocation once before fine-tuning. For IFCLoRA, the system first samples calibration examples from the training set with a fixed calibration seed. It then performs hook-based causal zero-ablation tracing on the frozen model to estimate task-conditioned influence strengths between modules. The final module importance score combines information-flow centrality and local gradient sensitivity. A temperature-scaled softmax maps the total rank budget to continuous quotas, and flooring plus residual balancing converts them to an integer rank allocation whose total rank units match the corresponding LoRA budget. Once the rank allocation is fixed, IFCLoRA follows the same fine-tuning process as standard LoRA.

Thus, IFCLoRA trades a moderate one-time preprocessing cost for more effective use of the same low-rank budget. 17 NeurIPS Paper Checklist
